## PART 1: The grocery experiment (0:35–2:00)

**[ON SCREEN: map with 4 city pins; shopping carts filling at the same time]**

In December 2025, Consumer Reports, Groundwork Collaborative and More Perfect Union ran a study on Instacart. They had 437 shoppers in four cities fill identical carts at the same store, during coordinated time windows.

**[STAT CARD]**
- **~75%** of items showed up at more than one price
- Some shoppers paid up to **23% more** for the same item
- Cart totals varied about **7%** on average
- Estimated cost for a family of four: about **$1,200 a year**

*Source: Groundwork Collaborative / Consumer Reports / More Perfect Union, Dec. 2025*

A box of saltines at a Target in Ohio: $3.99 for some people, $4.69 for others.

To be fair: Instacart said these were random price tests, not based on personal or demographic data, and the study didn't find a clear link to demographics. But after the report, Instacart **ended all item price tests** on its platform, and both the FTC and New York's attorney general started asking questions.

So here's the real question: if prices can already change from person to person, what happens when they're set using *your* data?

## PART 2: What "surveillance pricing" means (2:00–3:30)

**[ON SCREEN: a shopper's silhouette made of data points: location pin, phone model, browsing history, cart]**

That's what regulators now call **surveillance pricing**: using personal data to guess the highest price *you specifically* are willing to pay.

In January 2025, the FTC published early findings from a study of companies that build pricing tools for retailers, including Mastercard, Accenture, McKinsey and others.

**[STAT CARD: "WHAT CAN BE TRACKED"]**
- Your location
- Your browsing and purchase history
- Your **mouse movements** on a page
- Even **what you leave in your cart** without buying

*Source: FTC Surveillance Pricing Study, preliminary staff findings, Jan. 2025*

Think about what that means. Hesitating over the "buy" button is data. Coming back three times to the same product is data. Shopping from an expensive phone? Data.

**[ON SCREEN: same flight, two laptops, two prices. Text: "HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE"]**

Important: the FTC's findings were preliminary, and the examples it shared were hypothetical, because the company data is confidential. It's not proof that every store does this. It shows the tools exist and are being sold.

## PART 3: 2026 — the year states fought back (3:30–5:10)

**[ON SCREEN: US map, states lighting up one by one]**

Here's what's new. In 2026, states stopped just studying this and started banning it.

**[STATE CARD: MARYLAND]**
First ban, in effect since **October 1, 2026**. Large grocery stores and grocery delivery apps can't use personal data to set individual prices, and in-store prices must hold for at least one business day.
The catch: consumer groups point to loopholes, like **loyalty-program pricing**.
*Source: Maryland HB 895 (Protection From Predatory Pricing Act)*

**[STATE CARD: CONNECTICUT]**
Signed in 2026: bans surveillance pricing by retail sellers and food-delivery services, and requires an online label when personal data is used to raise a price.
*Source: Connecticut 2026 law (effective date per enacted text)*

**[STATE CARD: NEW JERSEY]**
Signed **July 23, 2026**: grocery stores and delivery apps can't vary prices based on personal data. It's the **first state where shoppers can sue** over it, with penalties up to **$50,000 per violation**. Stores have until **August 1, 2027** to comply. It also pauses new **electronic shelf labels**.
*Source: NJ Fair Price Protection Act, P.L.2026, c.55*

**[STATE CARD: NEW YORK]**
Doesn't ban it, but since November 2025 companies must show this exact sentence:
**"THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA."**
*Source: NY Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act*

And it's spreading: over **40 bills in 24+ states** were pending this summer.

**[ON SCREEN: FTC seal]**
At the federal level, in August 2026 the FTC proposed a policy saying hidden personalized pricing can be deceptive or unfair. It's a proposal, not a ban. The FTC itself says Congress hasn't given it power to outlaw the practice everywhere.

## PART 4: Electronic shelf labels (5:10–5:55)

**[ON SCREEN: grocery aisle; digital price tags flicker and change]**

Notice those small digital price tags showing up in stores? They let a store change prices across an entire aisle in seconds.

That isn't automatically bad. Stores say it saves labor. But it's why Maryland now requires grocery prices to hold for at least a business day, and why New Jersey paused new installations. Lawmakers are worried prices could change during the day, depending on who's in the store and when.

---

## PART 5: Five ways to protect your wallet (5:55–7:35)

**[ON SCREEN: checklist animating in]**

You can't turn this off completely. But you can make it much harder to target you.

**1. Compare before you buy.**
Check the same item logged out, in a private window, or on another device, or ask someone else to check it. If the price changes, you just learned something.

**2. Watch the cart-abandon game.**
If prices drop after you leave something in your cart, that's your data working. Be careful with that trick: prices can go *up* too.

**3. Know what your loyalty card costs.**
Discounts are real, but they're a data trade. And in Maryland, loyalty-program pricing is one of the loopholes.

**4. Look for the label.**
In New York, if you see *"This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data,"* you're seeing personalized pricing. Shop around.

**5. Report it.**
If you're in a state with a ban, report suspected violations to your **state attorney general**. In New Jersey, starting in 2027, shoppers can also sue.
